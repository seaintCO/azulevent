The founder’s guide to cap tables, dilution and control

Equity financing is about more than the valuation in a headline. Understand the ownership math and governance provisions before signing.

By Ocean Azul Partners Editorial · October 24, 2026 · 7 min read

Founders often celebrate a financing round by sharing the company valuation. Yet the amount of capital raised tells only part of the story. The full economic result depends on how new shares are issued, how employee options are treated, and what rights investors receive alongside their ownership.

A capitalization table records who owns which shares, options and other securities. It should also model instruments that may convert into equity later. A founder needs to understand both the ownership shown today and the fully diluted picture that could exist after future financing events.

Consider a simple illustration: a company worth $8 million before an investment raises $2 million of new money. If the terms are otherwise plain, the investor receives approximately 20 percent of the post-money equity. The founder group collectively owns less afterward, even though the business now has more resources.

Voting power and economic ownership are not always identical. Board seats, protective provisions, consent rights, liquidation preferences and transfer restrictions can influence decisions even when an investor does not hold a majority of the common shares.

Founders should ask what happens if growth is slower than planned, additional capital is required, a sale is proposed or the relationship with an investor changes. These questions are difficult to address after the relevant rights have already been granted.

The goal is not to avoid outside investment. It is to understand the trade: capital and partnership in exchange for defined economic and governance rights. A qualified startup attorney and a clear, scenario-based cap table are essential before any binding financing document is signed.
